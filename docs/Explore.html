Gravitational N-body simulation
Every particle attracts every other according to Newton's inverse-square law: F = G·m₁·m₂ / r². The force grows with mass and shrinks with the square of distance. No behaviour is scripted. Clusters, orbits, slingshots, and collisions emerge from this single rule applied to every pair at once.
Try “Two galaxies colliding” and watch tidal tails form as particles are flung outward during the merger. This is the same logic behind many real large-scale gravitational systems.
Each particle interacts with every other one, so larger systems create much richer motion.
Low G produces gentle drifting and loose clusters. High G creates violent collapses and ejections.
This sets the balance between collapse and expansion.
Different starting geometries reveal very different gravitational behaviour.
Longer trails make orbital paths and escapes easier to see.

The Central Limit Theorem
Take almost any population with a finite mean and variance. Draw a sample of size n, compute its average, and repeat that many times. The distribution of sample means moves toward a bell curve even if the original population is skewed or irregular.
This is one reason why confidence intervals, regression inference, and many statistical tests work so well in practice.
The left panel shows the population. The right panel shows the distribution of the sample means.
As n grows, the sampling distribution usually becomes more bell-shaped and narrower.

Coin tosses and the Law of Large Numbers
A fair coin is random in the short run, but the proportion of heads tends to settle near 0.5 as the number of tosses grows. This is the Law of Large Numbers in action. Small samples jump around wildly. Large samples stabilise.
You can also bias the coin. Then the long-run proportion settles near the true probability instead of 0.5.
This is the true probability used to generate each toss.
Use small batches to see volatility and large batches to see convergence.
The line tracks the cumulative proportion of heads over time.

Correlation and regression
Regression tries to describe the relationship between x and y with a line. Correlation measures how strongly the variables move together. Stronger relationships and less noise make the fitted line more stable and raise R².
This is a simple way to see that slope, correlation, and predictive fit are related but not identical ideas.
More points usually make the fitted pattern easier to estimate.
Positive slopes rise from left to right. Negative slopes fall.
Higher noise spreads the points farther from the line and lowers R².
This helps show when a straight-line regression fits well and when it misses the real structure.

Permutation test
A permutation test asks whether the observed difference between two groups is unusual under the null hypothesis of no real group effect. It does this by repeatedly shuffling the group labels and rebuilding the null distribution from the shuffled data.
If the observed difference sits far in the tail of the shuffled distribution, the result is unlikely under the null and the p-value becomes small.
Each group gets the same number of observations. Larger samples produce a tighter null distribution.
This shifts group B relative to group A. With effect 0, the groups come from the same distribution.
Higher noise creates more overlap and makes the observed difference less distinct.
More permutations make the null distribution smoother and the p-value estimate more stable.

Simpson's paradox
Simpson's paradox happens when the overall relationship in combined data points in one direction, while the relationship inside each subgroup points in another direction. It is a reminder that aggregation can hide important structure.
Use the controls to shift group sizes and group offsets. The subgroup lines can slope upward while the combined line slopes downward, or the reverse.
More points make the subgroup patterns easier to see.
This controls the direction inside each subgroup.
Larger offsets separate the groups more strongly and make the paradox easier to create.
Higher noise makes all fitted relationships less sharp.
